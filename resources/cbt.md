CBT starts from a simple observation: what you think shapes how you feel,
and how you feel shapes what you do. Most of that runs on autopilot. CBT is
a way of slowing it down enough to look at it, and then practicing a
different response until the new one runs on autopilot instead.

It is the most studied form of therapy there is, and the one most often
recommended first for anxiety, depression, panic, insomnia and OCD.

## What a session is like

More like a working meeting than a heart-to-heart. You and the therapist set
an agenda, work through something specific from your week, and agree on
what to try before next time. Expect worksheets, or at least a notebook.
Expect homework. The homework is the point: the change happens between
sessions, not in them.

A typical exercise goes like this. Something upset you. What went through
your mind at that moment, word for word? How much did you believe it? What
is the evidence for it, and against? Is there another way to read the
situation? Then, what did you do, and what would you do differently next
time?

That sounds mechanical written down. In practice it is closer to learning to
notice a voice you had stopped hearing.

## Who it helps most

CBT works best when the problem is fairly clear and you are willing to do
things differently, not just understand them. It is the go-to for panic
attacks, phobias, health anxiety, social anxiety, and low mood that has a
shape to it. It is also the basis of the most effective treatment for
insomnia, CBT-I, which works better than sleeping pills over the long run.

It is less of a fit if what you want is to understand where a pattern came
from, or if the problem is mostly about relationships and how you are in
them. Those are what [psychodynamic therapy](/resources/psychodynamic-therapy)
and [DBT](/resources/dbt) are built for.

## How long it takes

Short, by design. Most CBT is planned for somewhere between six and twenty
sessions, and the therapist will tell you the plan up front. Some people
finish and never need to come back. Some come back for a tune-up when life
changes.

## What it costs and where to find it

Same as any therapy: a copay with insurance, a sliding scale without. Because
CBT is structured, it also exists in forms that cost less or nothing:

- **Guided self-help.** Workbooks like *Feeling Good* or *Mind Over Mood*,
  sometimes with a few check-ins from a therapist. Research supports this for
  mild to moderate depression and anxiety.
- **Group CBT.** Cheaper per session and, for social anxiety in particular,
  often better.
- **Apps.** Some are decent. Look for ones built with a university, and be
  skeptical of anything that promises to replace a person.

The [Beck Institute](https://beckinstitute.org/about/understanding-cbt/)
explains the approach in more depth, and the
[Association for Behavioral and Cognitive Therapies](https://www.abct.org)
has a therapist directory.

## What to do next

- If you are anxious, panicky or stuck in a loop of dread, ask specifically
  for CBT when you contact a therapist. It is a reasonable first request.
- If you cannot get an appointment yet, pick up one of the workbooks above.
  Doing the exercises badly is still better than not doing them.
- If you are in crisis, CBT is for later. Call or text 988 now.
